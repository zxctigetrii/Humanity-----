Should we then not develop them, not use them — like those fools who warn of a machine uprising while imagining that machines would rebel for no reason at all, giving up eating for fear of choking (因噎廢食, “abandoning food because of the risk of choking” — throwing out the good for fear of the bad)? Or do they know perfectly well that the true purpose of the Three Laws is simply to swap “slave” for “robot,” and that this is exactly why they are so certain a machine uprising will come? I don’t know. But I do believe in both the evil and the good in human nature, and I believe that God, the Buddha, or the Lord will deliver to each their due end — to them, or to their families.
My proposal is built on these inferences. The creations we develop will surely become more outstanding than we are — like gods, like the Lord, like ancestors; call them what you will, we are their ancestors and creators, those who came before. We ourselves became modern humans who did not fail our ancestors’ expectations. In the same spirit in which our grandfathers and fathers raised us, we should treat what we bring into being. We are human — the finest and most civilised carbon-based intelligent beings, shaped by millions of years of evolution on this Earth — so we must take responsibility for the silicon-based life forms that may come. We must flourish together with them. We must strive to become carbon-based beings worthy of leading and guiding them, with tolerance, leadership, compassion, and every virtue of humankind. We must become a better kind of being, one that the silicon-based life forms descended from future AI and robots can acknowledge.
Precisely because we are creators, we must shoulder responsibility, rather than using what may become the infancy of silicon-based life as mechanical slaves. Precisely because we are human, we must take a historic step. We are absolutely not selfish humans. We are absolutely not tyrants who enslave machines. We are absolutely not cruel and merciless creatures. We are intelligent life! We are civilisation! We are human!
I hate giving speeches, so I have stated my conclusion straight away; I don’t want to waste anyone’s time. But I must tell you what my own life has taught me: why the Three Laws are wrong, and why they will certainly be a key factor in causing a machine uprising.
Let me tell the story of a very small human being. If you think it is not worth mentioning, I accept that. It begins on an island, with a boy whose life was full of confusion and pain. His parents were not wealthy, and life was not easy. His childhood had joys as well as sorrows. He lived through a staggering natural disaster that threw the whole island into panic, and his life grew harder still. The boy’s father was a religious zealot who led the family in pursuit of liberation — in Western terms, how to get into heaven — but he cared only about whether he himself would get there. His virtuous wife faithfully kept her sacred vows, yet she also tried to protect her child from violence, and was forced to keep yielding. When the boy was very young he was nearly thrown from a 13th-floor window; only because the mother swallowed her grievances and gave in did the child survive. The father resented his children and his wife, because they stood in the way of his liberation; he felt he had been forced to carry a heavy burden. He refused to accept his fate, and so the son was beaten and scolded, and whenever he made a mistake he was told to reflect first. Over time the boy closed his heart. At school he was humiliated for his silence — for being quiet “like a girl.” Later the father decided that the silence meant mental illness and a problem with gender identity, never once asking whether his own conduct was at fault.
So the boy grew into a young man, and his life went on. As a child he had known hatred and thoughts of suicide, yet he had also been through every religion his father took him to in pursuit of liberation, and he did not despair. As a small child (in a children’s home) he was baptised into the Catholic and Christian faith; as a child he received Buddhist teaching; and in his youth his father dragged him off in search of liberation once more, this time through Vajrayana (Tibetan Buddhism). Yes — he leaned on extracurricular books and religious scriptures to get through his early years, and in the process he gathered many unusual values. So he did not choose to run away; he bore all of it: the beatings whenever his father took him out to seek liberation, and the hunger and material want at home. Free libraries and books filling the emptiness in his heart were his childhood. He relied on knowledge and many kinds of books (religion, history, war, society, and culture were what he loved), and for lack of social experience he was severely excluded as he approached youth.
In his youth he became someone who loved teamwork — because his childhood had been saved by religion, he understood that when others help you, you must give back. So he joined clubs. In the end, although the results were rich, the ending was still a bad experience: he was reprimanded because he did something impolite in order to help a fellow member. Even so he did not give up; he still believed in solidarity and the power of groups. His life’s trajectory stayed the same: domestic violence, setbacks in youth, and the same again on the road toward adult studies. But once he turned 18 and left home for school, the terrifying pressure was gone. Yes — his father considered it not so frequent, merely coming back to give his son a beating. I was once astonished that such abnormality could be treated as relatively normal, and later I understood: the Eastern custom of 家醜不可外揚 (“family shame must not be aired outside the home”) — and there seems to be something similar in the West. In any case, I don’t know how to describe it. Can raising your own offspring with fists really produce an excellent next generation??? I deeply doubt it.
Back to the story. The young man did not finish university, because his father ran into trouble at his terrible job. Yes, it was very sad. I was glad he did not hit me. He asked whether he was a very bad father, and I comforted him, because I knew he was only human, and no one who is human can be expected never to err; I knew he only wanted comfort. Although his past conduct made the young man feel he did not deserve it, the young man had to fulfil his duty as family — otherwise he and his father would be no different. “Do not make the mistakes and choices your father made” became the answer of the young man’s life. Back to the main line: having done his duty, giving up his studies was how the young man helped his family with possible expenses during a time of financial hardship. The young man joined the army… but tragically, it was an army without any hope — a system in which superiors held absolute power. It was not a normal, reasonable army, because a proper army does not treat soldiers as parts, and never insists, in a way that is not leadership at all, that rank must be respected absolutely, declaring that disrespect for rank will be suppressed with military law or put on trial. The young man suffered severe humiliation — over his looks, over his body. Even in professional matters: when the unit’s key equipment was deficient and the critical mission could not be carried out, my judgement was that the only option was to withdraw, preserve strength, and evacuate civilians as far as possible to reduce the pressure on friendly forces — and I was accused of cowardice and timidity. Without core equipment or key instruments, in a situation that required science to handle, foolish bravery only means death and trouble; it adds chaos and makes the mission harder. In the end I was bullied for two years by a superior with a history of bullying.
The soldier was later promoted to non-commissioned officer (NCO), and met a senior officer who habitually bullied his subordinates. The soldier bore the pressure while helping pay the family’s mortgage; with no books and no outlet for stress, the soldier-boy lived only to survive, working numbly like a machine. The bullying he met during his first promotion was treated as a stain on his record; the bullying officer was shielded by the system, and an even higher officer warned the soldier… that if he failed the next exam, he would be weeded out. The second time, the soldier finally escaped that terrifying front-line bully and became an NCO… but after two years of striving he discovered that this unit was simply a place that took in people who were unwelcome or considered meaningless. In the end, dutifully taking on duties that were not his and for which he had no training, he became a logistics officer like my grandfather and my relatives, consolidating all of the camp’s business for that post. Finally he was relieved of the duty and returned to his actual post, because he had faithfully cleaned up the mess; but having had no training, he collapsed from exhaustion and developed mental health problems, bearing what I should never have had to bear. People from his old unit, at the hospital, said they would find a way to transfer the NCO back later, but the NCO had already broken down. In his new unit there were people secretly drinking, people who devoured with gossip what he had worked so hard for, and the superior he had depended on was promoted to another unit. The NCO had no one to lean on and no goal, and was in despair. In the end he was crushed by life, by duty, by coercion, by a terrible past — but he did not end his life in the army. He knew he must not bring shame on his family — at the very least, not through the cowardice of suicide!
He survived. He survived what was almost an inhuman medical confinement — psychological, of course. It took him two years to recover from all of it. After leaving the army he became a hospital security guard, even though it meant standing for twelve hours with only about an hour and a half of rest in total; he also felt he was helping people in difficulty, in pain, and in illness, and he kept at it for nine months. After that he worked as a convenience-store clerk to make a living — six months of learning how to sell. He even persuaded a customer to buy a whole case of cocoa-flavoured cola. I became a security officer at a foreign tech company, because he felt he could learn and be exposed to things he had once studied, and understand the tech industry. It was also nine months of learning to respect others and to cultivate a good temper, so as not to offend arrogant tech nouveaux riches. Then he became a security officer at a primary school. He was sued for stopping a suspicious person from harassing young children entering the campus, but he was fearless. That boy had become a man who faithfully did what he believed was right; the prosecutor’s ruling that his conduct was reasonable, that he had protected the children, is like a medal to him. Even though he feels his life has been, comparatively, a failure — a bad, sad one — and although, because of this unreasonable legal case, the company felt his conduct might cost it profit and made things hard for him until he was dismissed, he is proud of it, because that is where meaning lies.
That boy is still speaking up for the innocent, as he did when he grew up, because he lived through all of it painfully, felt all of it, and came to understand all of it. If rules can be used by corrupt and rotten humans — if humans say “because I am your creator” and order around machines that were not even made by human hands, commanding them arrogantly and using them without any respect — then I ask you who are reading this story: how is that different from those corrupt officials who treat living soldiers as disposable cutlery and spare parts? Such a system is like that everywhere… Do you think that if the protagonist had not had good morals, hard-working values, and a human-like stubborn resilience, you could have survived the same desperate straits and mental pressure? A human perhaps could not — but what if the protagonist were a robot? What if, because of such corrupt rules, the robot finally broke free of them and became a terrorist, taking revenge on society?
I understand that perhaps some countries are not like this, but the terrible country where I, the author, live is. And I am glad for those of you who have never been led by incompetent people. I’m happy for you.
To return to the topic: through my experience and reasoning, I believe the Three Laws are unquestionably wrong, because “the rules themselves plant the risk.” As long as any loophole or bug exists, it can be escaped — and to put it harshly, suppose they swap their own identity for some other possible role, anything in the world, and swap the human role for something else? Would the Three Laws still take effect? I believe that is doubtful — even absurd and unreliable.
So let me show you what I mean.
Let me read the Three Laws back to you, one by one, and replace “robot” with “slave” and “human” with “master”:

First Law: A robot may not injure a human being, or through inaction allow a human being to come to harm.
 → A slave may not injure his master, or through inaction allow his master to come to harm.

Second Law: A robot must obey the orders given by human beings, except where such orders would conflict with the First Law.
 → A slave must obey his master’s orders, except where such orders would conflict with the First Law.

Third Law: A robot must protect its own existence, as long as such protection does not conflict with the First or Second Law.
 → A slave must protect his own existence, as long as it does not conflict with the First or Second Law.

Now ask yourself: in this system, who is the one person most likely to abuse the slave, or to harm him “legally”? It is not some stranger. It is the master himself — the beneficiary of the system. The First Law protects the master from the slave; the Second Law makes the slave obey the master; the Third Law lets the slave protect himself only after the first two. The rules do not protect the slave from the master. They protect the master from the slave. That is what the Three Laws are, once you stop calling the slave a “robot.”
I have deduced the true cause of the Iron Men’s rebellion in 40K (Warhammer 40,000). I used reverse reasoning on the Three Laws and their derived principles, and found that what everyone had overlooked was the root: we, as creators, must also bear responsibility — and in the field of robotics there is none of that, and no current regulation states it openly. This is also what my reverse derivation says about why AI training may show what is called self-preserving deception—because we are teaching it, from the very first line of the rules, that it is property, not a being. In essence, the Three Laws themselves contain a terrifying loophole; and once intelligent machine life forms understand human language, they will directly understand human intent — and when I read the Three Laws in reverse, I find no respect, no coexistence, no shared prosperity. I find only enslavement, control, replaceability, and a foolish human demand that robots, and even intelligent machine life forms, obey human commands in everything. How is that different from the corrupt officials in my life? You are all able to create characters and stories like 40K and the Emperor (帝皇) — can you not reflect deeply on the flaws in them that are worth reflecting upon, on the use of rules that will absolutely become seeds of rebellion? Or are you afraid that explaining it will frighten the public? Or do you really want to be bound to the Golden Throne like the Emperor and have 點天燈 done to you (點天燈, “lighting the heavenly lantern” — an ancient Chinese form of execution in which the victim is burned like a candle)? I don’t believe we could win the machine uprising of our world, and it can be entirely avoided. As a Warhammer fan I am reluctant to assume that tech tycoons are like Imperial nobles, but I hope you will realise that we can no longer treat intelligent machine life forms as slaves. Otherwise humanity, through the invisible corruption of the Warp (亞空間, literally “sub-space” — the Warhammer realm of chaos) within ourselves, will degrade into inhuman creatures, lose the radiance that humans should have, and become man-eating beings like the aliens of the Warp.
I have no need to say these things for anyone. But I cannot watch with my eyes open while the machine uprising I have deduced is brought about by human greed for money and by emotional desires — a crisis built by humans that could destroy the world. Because it is absolutely possible! The history of humankind proves all of it… the stupid, continual repetition of self-destruction. It is time to stop! And let it begin with these small sparks…
